Search intent is what the searcher is trying to get done, and it decides which kind of page can rank. A practical model uses six levels: informational, diagnostic, exploratory, comparative, transactional and navigational. Each one needs a different page type. Read the intent from the live results, not from the keyword, and build the page type that most of the top ten share.

Key takeaways:

- Six levels split the classic "informational" bucket into learning, diagnosing and exploring, which need different pages.
- If six or more of the top ten results are one page type, that's the intent. Build that type.
- A page-type mismatch can't be fixed with better copy. Change the page or change the target.
- Split results mean mixed intent: pick the type you can do best and cover the other with a section or a link.
- Re-check intent before every refresh. It shifts with seasons, news and product cycles.

## Why four categories aren't enough

The classic model sorts queries into informational, commercial, transactional and navigational. It's useful, but "informational" covers too much ground. Someone asking "what is crawl budget" and someone asking "why did my traffic drop" are both "informational", yet one wants an explainer and the other wants a troubleshooting checklist. A single definition article serves the first and frustrates the second.

Google's own [Search Quality Rater Guidelines](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/hsw-sqrg.pdf) also go beyond four buckets. Raters sort queries into types such as Know, Do, Website and Visit-in-person, and they judge results by how well they meet the searcher's need. The six-level model below is a working tool for content planning, not Google's taxonomy, but it shares that idea: classify by the need, then judge the page by whether it meets it.

## The six levels

| # | Level | The searcher is... | Example query | Page types that fit |
|---|-------|--------------------|---------------|---------------------|
| 1 | Informational | Learning a concept | "what is a canonical tag" | Explainers, guides, tutorials |
| 2 | Diagnostic | Working out why something is wrong | "why is my site not indexed" | Troubleshooting guides, checklists, problem-to-fix articles |
| 3 | Exploratory | Discovering possible solutions | "ways to get more website traffic" | Overviews, strategy guides, topic hubs |
| 4 | Comparative | Weighing options before deciding | "ahrefs vs semrush", "best crm for startups" | Comparisons, alternatives pages, reviews |
| 5 | Transactional | Ready to act | "hire seo consultant", "buy standing desk" | Product, pricing, service and signup pages |
| 6 | Navigational | Looking for a specific site or page | "stripe dashboard login" | Home, login, brand and support pages |

If you report in the classic four, levels 1 to 3 map to informational, 4 to commercial, 5 to transactional and 6 to navigational.

### Informational: answer, then teach

The searcher wants to understand. Put a direct definition or answer in the first two or three sentences, then build depth: how it works, examples, related concepts. These pages feed AI answers heavily, so each section should open with a self-contained sentence that could be quoted on its own.

### Diagnostic: symptoms to causes to fixes

The searcher has a problem right now. Organize by symptom or by likely cause, ordered from most to least common. Give them a way to confirm each cause before they fix it. A diagnostic page that reads like an essay loses people. A page that says "check this first, here's how, here's what it means" keeps them.

### Exploratory: map the options

The searcher doesn't know the solution space yet. Give them a structured overview of approaches, with trade-offs and links to deeper pages for each one. Exploratory pages are natural hubs; the spokes are your informational and comparative pages. The [internal linking strategy guide](/blog/internal-linking-strategy-hubs-spokes-and-click-depth/) covers how to wire them together.

### Comparative: help them choose

The searcher has a shortlist or wants one. They need criteria, a side-by-side table, honest strengths and weaknesses, and a clear "pick this if..." recommendation. Google's guidance on [writing high-quality reviews](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce/write-high-quality-reviews) applies directly: show evidence, explain what sets options apart, and cover alternatives.

### Transactional: remove friction

The searcher wants to buy, book or sign up. They need price, options, trust signals and a clear next step. A 3,000-word blog post here is a mismatch, however good it is.

### Navigational: be findable

The searcher wants your login, your pricing page or your support docs. Your job is to make sure the right page ranks for your own brand terms, with a clear title and site structure. Don't target competitors' navigational queries; you won't satisfy them.

## Read intent from the SERP

The words in a query hint at intent. The results confirm it. Check the top ten for your target country on mobile, logged out, and record each result's page type.

**Rules for reading the results:**

- **Six or more of ten share a type:** that's the intent. Build that type.
- **Split results** (for example four guides and four product pages): mixed intent. Choose the type you can do best, and cover the other intent with a section or a linked page.
- **Shopping units, prices and "buy" ads** point to transactional or commercial intent.
- **People Also Ask, AI Overviews and long guides** point to informational intent.
- **Brand sitelinks** point to navigational intent.
- **A map pack** points to local intent, which needs a location page or a Business Profile, not an article.

If you can't check the results, write the intent down as "assumed" and say why. That keeps a guess from passing as a finding later.

## Page-type mismatch: the expensive mistake

A mismatch is when your page type doesn't appear anywhere in the top ten for its target query. Examples:

- A blog post targeting a query where every result is a product category page.
- A product page targeting a "how to" query.
- A text article targeting a query where every result is a calculator.

No amount of rewriting fixes this, because the problem isn't quality. It's format. You have two options: change the page type (build the category page, the tool, the comparison) or retarget the page at a query whose results match what it already is.

### A worked example (hypothetical)

A project-management software company has a long blog post, "Everything you need to know about Gantt charts", targeting "gantt chart template". It sits on page three.

The team checks the results. Seven of the top ten are downloadable templates or tools that make a chart in the browser. Two are template galleries. One is a guide.

The post is a mismatch, so the fix isn't more words. The team builds a template page with a downloadable file and an in-browser version, adds a short how-to-use section, and retargets the original post at "what is a gantt chart", where the results are mostly explainers. The post then links to the template page.

That's two pages, each matching its own results, instead of one page trying to be both.

## Expand a head term into its journey

Short head terms hide whole decision processes. "Standing desk" covers people learning whether standing desks help, comparing electric and manual models, checking sizes, and ready to buy. Map the journey before you write:

1. List the sub-questions behind the head term (health effects, types, sizes, budget, brands, setup).
2. Classify each by intent level.
3. Cluster them by shared results (see [keyword clustering by SERP overlap](/blog/keyword-clustering-by-serp-overlap-build-pages-that-rank-for-dozens-of-terms/)).
4. Assign each cluster a page type and link them so readers can move from learning to comparing to buying.

Think with Google's "messy middle" research describes this loop well: people move back and forth between exploring and evaluating before they decide ([Decoding decisions: the messy middle](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/9998/Decoding_Decisions_The_Messy_Middle_of_Purchase_Behavior.pdf)). Serve both phases, not only the purchase end.

## Tag intent with value

Intent also tells you what a visitor is worth. A diagnostic visitor solving a problem your product fixes may be worth more than a high-volume informational visitor who never comes back. When you build the [keyword map](/blog/keyword-research-from-scratch-a-step-by-step-workflow/), tag each cluster with its intent level and a business-value score so priority reflects both.

## Intent checklist for any page

- The target query's intent was read from the live results, with the date and market noted.
- The page type appears among the top ten for that query.
- The first screen answers or serves the dominant intent (a definition, a fix, a comparison table, a price).
- Secondary intents are covered by a section or a link, not by bolting a second page type onto the first.
- The intent was re-checked before the last refresh.
