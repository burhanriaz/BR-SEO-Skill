Information gain is the useful information a page adds beyond what the pages already ranking for the same query provide. A page that rewrites the top three results gives the reader nothing new, so there is little reason for it to outrank them. To beat them, find what they all miss and supply it with something you can uniquely provide: first-hand testing, your own data, a worked example, a decision framework, or newer and better-sourced facts.

Key takeaways:

- If your page could be assembled from the top five results, it has no information gain.
- Start with a gap audit: list what every top result covers, then what none of them do.
- The strongest gains come from things only you can produce: data, tests, experience, tools.
- Put the gain early on the page and say what it is. Buried originality doesn't help.
- Information gain also makes a page more likely to be cited by AI answers, which favor specific, verifiable facts.

## Where the idea comes from

The phrase "information gain" became popular in SEO partly because of a Google patent, [Contextual estimation of link information gain](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11354342B2), which describes scoring documents by how much new information they offer a user who has already seen others. A patent shows an idea Google explored, not a confirmed ranking signal, so don't build a strategy on the patent itself.

You don't need it anyway. Google's public guidance says the same thing in plain words. Its [helpful content self-assessment](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks: "Does the content provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis?" and "Does the content provide substantial value when compared to other pages in search results?" And its [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) list "stitching or combining content from different web pages without adding value" as an example of scaled content abuse.

So whatever the mechanism, the direction is clear: add something the other results don't have.

## Step 1: Run a gap audit

Search your target query (your market, mobile, logged out) and open the top five organic results. Build a coverage table.

| Subtopic or element | Result 1 | Result 2 | Result 3 | Result 4 | Result 5 |
|---------------------|:--------:|:--------:|:--------:|:--------:|:--------:|
| Definition | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Step-by-step process | yes | yes | no | yes | yes |
| Cost ranges | no | yes | no | no | no |
| Worked example with real numbers | no | no | no | no | no |
| Common mistakes | yes | no | yes | no | yes |
| Original data or test | no | no | no | no | no |
| Updated in the last year | no | yes | no | yes | no |

Now sort the rows into three groups:

- **Table stakes:** covered by four or five results. Your page needs these too, briefly and well.
- **Weak spots:** covered by one or two results, poorly or with stale facts. You can win these by doing them properly.
- **Gaps:** covered by none. This is where information gain lives.

Add the questions from People Also Ask, forums and your own support inbox that none of the results answer. Those are often the best gaps of all, because real people are asking them.

## Step 2: Pick your source of gain

Not every gap is worth filling. Pick the ones that matter to the reader and that you can fill credibly. Common sources, roughly from strongest to weakest:

| Source of gain | What it looks like | Effort |
|----------------|--------------------|:------:|
| Original data | A survey of your customers, aggregated product usage, your own measurements | High |
| First-hand testing | "We ran these five tools on the same site and recorded the results" | High |
| Experience from doing the work | What actually happened on a real project, including what went wrong | Medium |
| A tool or calculator | Lets the reader apply the advice to their own numbers | Medium to high |
| A decision framework | A table or flowchart that tells the reader what to choose for their situation | Medium |
| Worked examples | The method applied to a concrete case, step by step, with numbers | Low to medium |
| Expert input | A quote or review from a named, qualified person | Low to medium |
| Better sourcing | Primary sources where others cite roundups; corrected errors | Low |
| Newer facts | Current versions, prices, rules, with dates | Low |

The best gains are hard to copy. A competitor can match your outline in an afternoon. They can't match your test results without running the test.

Be honest about what you have. If you haven't tested something, don't imply you have. Google's guidance on [writing high-quality reviews](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce/write-high-quality-reviews) asks for evidence of first-hand experience, and invented experience is a trust failure, not a gain. A clearly labeled hypothetical example is fine. A fake case study isn't.

## Step 3: Build the page around the gain

Gains help only if readers and engines notice them.

- **Say it early.** Mention the original element in the opening: "We tested..." or "Using data from 200 support tickets..." Readers should know within seconds why this page differs.
- **Give it a heading.** Put the test, the data or the framework under its own descriptive heading so it can be found and quoted.
- **Show the method.** How you collected the data, when, and with what limits. Method is what makes original data trustworthy.
- **Keep table stakes short.** Cover the basics clearly but briefly so the original material isn't buried under a rewrite of everyone else's.
- **Make facts explicit.** Numbers, dates, names. Specific, verifiable statements are what AI answers can ground and cite. The guide to [answer-first content](/blog/writing-answer-first-content-that-ai-engines-quote/) covers structuring those passages.

## A worked example (hypothetical)

A company that makes accounting software wants to rank for "how long to keep business receipts". The top five results all list the general retention rules and link to the tax authority. None explains what to do with digital copies, none covers the case where a business switched accounting systems, and none gives a simple decision table by document type.

The team's gain:

1. A decision table by document type and situation, each row linked to the official rule.
2. A section on digital copies and system migrations, written with input from the company's support team, who handle these questions every week.
3. A short note on what support tickets show people get wrong, described qualitatively because the team hasn't done a formal count.

Table stakes (the general rules) take one short section. The decision table and the migration section get their own headings near the top. A qualified accountant reviews the page because the topic involves tax. This is an illustration of the approach, not a real page or result.

## Step 4: Check you actually added something

Before publishing, test the draft:

- **The swap test.** Could a reader get everything on this page from the top five results? If yes, there's no gain.
- **The one-sentence test.** Can you say in one sentence what this page offers that the others don't? Put that sentence in the brief and near the top of the page.
- **The evidence test.** Is the gain backed by something checkable: a method, a source, a named reviewer, screenshots?
- **The relevance test.** Does the gain help with the searcher's actual task, or is it a tangent added to look original?

Building the gain into the [content brief](/blog/how-to-write-a-content-brief-writers-can-actually-use/) before writing starts is far easier than retrofitting it. And first-hand evidence doubles as an experience signal, which the [E-E-A-T guide](/blog/e-e-a-t-in-practice-signals-you-can-add-this-week/) covers.

## Information gain and AI search

AI answer engines summarize what's commonly said. If your page says only what's commonly said, the summary doesn't need you. Pages with distinctive, specific information (a number nobody else has, a clear comparison, a tested result) give an engine a reason to cite them by name. That's the same logic behind [generative engine optimization](/blog/generative-engine-optimization-geo-how-to-get-cited-by-ai-search/): be the source of something, not a summary of something.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding length instead of information.** More words on the same subtopics isn't gain.
- **Originality for its own sake.** A quirky angle that doesn't help the searcher doesn't count.
- **Burying the gain.** Original data in paragraph 14 might as well not exist.
- **Faking experience.** Invented tests, quotes or case studies can sink the page's trust entirely.
- **Skipping table stakes.** A page full of novel insight that omits the basic answer still fails the searcher.
