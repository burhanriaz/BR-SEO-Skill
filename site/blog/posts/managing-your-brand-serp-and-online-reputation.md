Your brand SERP is the first page of Google results when someone searches your company or product name, and for many prospects it is the first impression they get. Managing it means making sure that page is accurate and complete: your own site and sitelinks on top, a correct knowledge panel or Business Profile, your real profiles, and fair third-party coverage. You cannot delete criticism you dislike, but you can make sure the truth is well represented and respond in a way that does not boost the negative page.

Key takeaways:

- Audit your brand SERP monthly, signed out and on mobile, for the brand name and common variations.
- Win the basics first: a clear home page, `Organization` and `WebSite` markup, a claimed knowledge panel or Business Profile.
- Fill the page with results you control or earned: profiles, documentation, coverage, reviews.
- When responding to a negative listing, do not repeat your brand name or link to it; answer on properties you control.
- Google removes content only for specific policy reasons. "It's unfair" is not one of them.

## What a healthy brand SERP looks like

For a brand search, the searcher usually wants to find you, check you out, or do something specific (log in, get support, see pricing). A healthy result page supports all three.

| Position | Ideal result |
|----------|--------------|
| Top | Your home page, with sitelinks to the main sections |
| Side or top panel | Knowledge panel (organizations) or Business Profile (local businesses) with correct facts |
| Next results | Key owned pages: product, pricing, support, about |
| Mid page | Official profiles you maintain: social, video, app stores, code repositories |
| Lower | Third-party coverage: reviews, news, comparison articles, directories |
| Anywhere | Accurate answers in AI Overviews or AI Mode when they appear |

What signals a problem: a competitor's ad or comparison page outranking your home page, an outdated profile with old facts, a complaint thread in the top five, a wrong knowledge panel, or nothing at all except your site.

## Step 1: Audit it

Search your brand name, then variations: brand plus "reviews," "pricing," "login," "complaints," "vs" and your main product names. Use a private window, check on mobile, and note the location you are searching from.

Record each result in a sheet:

| Column | Example |
|--------|---------|
| Query | brand reviews |
| Position | 4 |
| URL | the result |
| Owner | Us, third party, competitor |
| Control | Full, partial (profile we manage), none |
| Sentiment | Positive, neutral, negative |
| Accurate? | Yes, outdated, wrong |
| Action | Update profile, ask for correction, build page, monitor |

Repeat the same queries in two or three AI assistants and record how they describe you. Wrong facts there usually trace back to a source you can find and fix.

## Step 2: Fix your own foundation

Most brand SERP problems start on your own site.

- **A clear home page.** State what you are, in plain words, near the top. That text often becomes your snippet and feeds how others describe you.
- **`WebSite` structured data** on the home page with `name` (and `alternateName` if needed). Google's [site names documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/site-names) calls it the strongest signal for the name it shows.
- **`Organization` structured data** with logo, contact points and `sameAs` links to profiles you control. The [Organization and WebSite schema guide](/blog/organization-and-website-schema-the-foundation-every-site-needs/) covers the markup.
- **Clear navigation and page titles**, which shape sitelinks.
- **Real contact details**: an address and a phone that is answered, not just a form. They are trust signals for users and engines alike.

## Step 3: Claim the panels

**Knowledge panel.** If one appears for your organization, you can claim it through Google's [verification process](https://support.google.com/knowledgepanel/answer/7534902): find the panel, select "Claim this knowledge panel," and sign in with an official site or profile linked to the entity, such as Search Console or YouTube. Once verified you can suggest corrections with supporting links. Not every panel can be claimed.

**Business Profile.** Local businesses that serve customers at a location or in a service area should use a Google Business Profile instead. Keep categories, hours and contact details accurate, and respond to reviews. The [Business Profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist/) covers it step by step.

## Step 4: Fill the page with accurate results

The brand SERP has a limited number of slots. Each accurate, useful result you add is a slot that represents you correctly.

1. **Official profiles** on the platforms your audience uses. Complete them, link them from your site, and keep facts consistent.
2. **Useful owned pages** that match brand-plus queries: pricing, support, documentation, status, careers, about.
3. **Earned coverage**: interviews, data studies cited by the press, conference talks. The [brand mentions guide](/blog/brand-mentions-vs-links-in-the-age-of-ai-search/) covers how coverage builds both links and recognition.
4. **Review platforms** relevant to your field, with a real program for asking satisfied customers to review you within each platform's rules.

Avoid thin "reputation" microsites and fake profiles made only to push results down. They are low-value pages created to manipulate results, and they tend to look exactly like what they are.

## Step 5: Handle negative results

### Decide whether to respond

| Type of result | Response |
|----------------|----------|
| Fair criticism of a real problem | Fix the problem; respond publicly on the platform if it allows replies |
| Outdated complaint about a fixed issue | Reply on the platform with what changed; ask the site owner to update |
| Factually wrong article | Ask the publisher for a correction with evidence |
| Review that breaks platform rules | Report it through that platform's process |
| Personal information, doxxing, explicit content | Use Google's removal request processes |
| Defamation | Get legal advice; do not argue in public |

### The rebuttal rule

When you respond to a negative page anywhere, **do not repeat your brand name, product keywords or URL** more than you must, and do not link to the negative page. Every mention and link you add tells search engines that page is relevant to your brand, which helps it rank. Address the issue on properties you control instead: a clear support page, an updated FAQ, a public changelog or status post that explains what was fixed.

### What Google will and will not remove

Google's [removal policies](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/9673730) cover specific categories: personal contact details, government ID numbers, financial information, doxxing, private records and some image types, among others. Its "Results about you" tool helps individuals find and request removal of results showing their contact details. Removing a result from Google does not remove it from the site hosting it. Negative opinions about a business are generally not removable through these policies.

If a page has already been changed or deleted but Google still shows the old version, use the refresh outdated content request rather than waiting for a recrawl.

## Step 6: Monitor

- Re-run the brand SERP audit monthly and after any launch, rebrand, incident or press cycle.
- Set up mention alerts for your brand and key people.
- Watch Search Console queries containing your brand: falling clicks with steady impressions can mean something above your listing is taking attention.
- Re-check AI assistant descriptions quarterly with the same prompts.

The [entity SEO guide](/blog/entity-seo-help-search-engines-and-llms-understand-your-brand/) covers the knowledge-graph side, which determines much of what shows in panels and AI answers.

## Common mistakes

- **Ignoring the brand SERP because "we rank first for our name."** The rest of the page still shapes the decision.
- **Arguing in the comments of a hit piece** with your brand name in every sentence.
- **Fake reviews or astroturfed profiles.** They violate platform policies and are easy to spot.
- **Inconsistent facts** across profiles, which confuse knowledge panels and AI answers.
- **No owned page for common brand questions**, such as pricing or "is it safe," leaving third parties to answer them.
